# Shaheen 1982 — reading the book By-Law 4.1.1 names

*21 August 2026 · from `Family Tree/Literature/` in the AFRP Management Drive*
*Red-teamed three times. Two headline findings were withdrawn; both retractions are on the screen.*

By-Law 4.1.1 admits a Regular Member of origin from ancestral Ramallah
**“as defined within Aziz Shaheen’s book entitled ‘Ramallah’”**. The by-law does not
paraphrase the book, summarise it, or list the families it contains. It delegates the
whole eligibility test to a single 1982 volume — *Ramallah: Its History and Its
Genealogies*, Azeez Shaheen, English text by Naseeb Shaheen, Birzeit University, 894
pages — which has been sitting in AFRP’s own Drive folder the whole time.

**A caution that governs everything below.** The book states no test — of any kind. Its
preface gives its purpose as memorial: so that Ramallah’s people “can always remember
their background.” 4.1.1 borrowed a memorial genealogy and made it a gate. Every
difficulty that follows is the by-law’s doing, not the book’s.

**A note on method.** The Arabic OCR is unusable and the English OCR interleaves the
columns on every two-column page. Every quotation below was read from the **page image**.
Anyone working from the text layer will reconstruct these passages wrongly — the first
version of this note did.

---

## 1 · The founder structure, in the book’s own words

> “The land was divided in the following manner among Rashed’s sons: Haddad, Ibrahim,
> Jirius, Shukair, and Hassaan each took one-fifth of the area of Ramallah…
>
> Haddad’s fifth was in turn divided equally between his five sons. Esa, Khaleel, Yousef,
> ‘Awwaad, and ‘Azzouz each got one-fifth.
>
> **Thus, Ramallah was formed by eight clans**, four clans of Haddad’s sons called **El
> Hadadeh** (since the ‘Azzouz and Jaghab clans combined into one clan), and four clans of
> their uncles, Ibrahim, Jirius, Shukair, and Hassaan. These were called **El Hamayel**.”
>
> — p. 22

Haddad’s sons and the clans they head: Esa → **Sharaka** (they lived in the eastern,
*shark*, part of town); Khaleel → **Jaghab**; Yousef → **Yousef**; ‘Awwaad → **‘Awwaad**;
‘Azzouz → ‘Azzouz, which merged.

Shaheen argues with his own sources in front of the reader — he disagrees with Joseph
Kaddoura’s 1954 account on whether the girl at the centre of the founding quarrel was
Sabra’s daughter or Rashed’s, and on who returned to El Karak, and concludes that Rashed’s
eldest son was named **Haddad**, not Sabra. That is a reason to trust the book and a reason
not to treat it as a register.

## 2 · The Azzouz question, closed

AFRP’s *Clan Family Roster* prints **Azzouz** as a family name under **Jaghab**; the
genealogy database issues **AZZ** references to **581** people as though it were a clan.
The platform had both on screen and declined to choose.

The book settles it twice. p. 22: the ‘Azzouz and Jaghab **clans** combined into one. And
p. 33, “The ‘Azzouz Incident” — a revenge party misdirected to the wrong quarter of town:

> “several ‘Azzouz men were slain, rather than his real relatives, the Shakara. **This is
> what caused the ‘Azzouz clan, on seeing their numbers diminished, to combine with the
> Jaghab clan.**”

So ‘Azzouz **was** a clan, with its own fifth of Haddad’s fifth of the land, and stopped
being one. The lineage deserves its reference series; the roster is right that Azzouz is
today a family within Jaghab. Ramallah has **eight** clans and **nine** reference prefixes.

The figures, stated properly: **581** people carry an AZZ primary reference, **94** carry
the Azzouz surname, **92** are in both. The surname count was never a subset of the
reference count, and an earlier draft that wrote “95 of 581” was wrong twice over — the
95 was a substring match folding in one *Abu-Azzouz*.

**But the database never re-coded the merger.** Of the 94 people surnamed Azzouz, 92 hold
an AZZ reference and exactly **one** of the 3,303 people with a Jaghab reference does. The
book records what happened around 1820; the roster records where the family sits today,
under Jaghab; the database records the descent line, which never stopped being its own.
Three sources, three time horizons, none of them wrong — and reading any one as the whole
answer is what made this look like a contradiction.

**An earlier draft also said “‘Azzouz is not a clan.”** That contradicts the book on the
very page it quoted. Corrected.

## 3 · Chapter 2 — “Outsiders Who Settled in Ramallah”

Four sentences in Chapter 2 attach an arriving family to a named clan. Verified from the
page images of printed pp. 25–26:

| family | clan | arrived | page |
|---|---|---|---|
| ‘Ajlouny | Jaghab | about 1750 | 25 |
| Nazzal (from Rafidia) | Sharaka | about 1805 | 26 |
| ‘Araj | Sharaka | about 1810 | 26 |
| Zagroot | Sharaka | about 1810 | 26 |
| Shahla | Sharaka | about 1810 | 26 |
| ‘Audi el Debbeeny | ‘Awwaad | 1855–60 | 26 |

Four sentences, six families, three clans. AFRP’s roster puts all six under exactly those
clans.

**One of the six rests on a transliteration.** The book’s ‘Audi el Debbeeny is the roster’s
*Eadeh* or its *Odeh*. Both sit under Awad, so the clan agrees either way — but in the
record the two diverge sharply (Eadeh is overwhelmingly Awad, Odeh overwhelmingly Jirius),
so this is a real choice, and it is ours, not the document’s.

**Who is not counted, and why.** The **Hishmehs** are given no clan — Khaleel came from
Burkeen and married an Ayyoub–Mikhail daughter, and the book stops there. The **Beders**,
the second **Nazzals** and the **Bajjalis** came from Beit Jala and “returned to Beit
Jala”. **El Ja’ooni** arrived in 1923 and is the first Muslim to settle permanently; the
eight clans are Greek Orthodox families. None is on the ledger in either direction.

**A disclosure the by-law does not make.** Of Kaddoura’s 1954 *History of Ramallah*,
Shaheen writes: “His is a very good book and **I have made extensive use of it in Chapters
2 to 4** of my brief history.” The chapter carrying the whole weight of the attachment
reading is, by the author’s own statement, substantially a restatement of a different 1954
book — one 4.1.1 does not name.

### Two readings of 4.1.1, and the book supports both

**Reading A — a family can be of Ramallah by attachment.** Chapter 2 says six families
“became part of” a named clan. AFRP’s own roster lists all six inside those clans. The
1944 census counts the eight clans at 4,885 and “outsiders living in Ramallah” at 1,415
*separately* — and the attached families are inside the clan figure. The book is the
by-law’s authority, and Chapter 2 is in the book.

**Reading B — origin means where the family came from.** The chapter is titled “**Outsiders**
Who Settled in Ramallah”. “The first **outsider** to come to Ramallah was Khaleel
‘Ajlouny.” p. 23: the town “attracted other families who joined the town’s **original
settlers**” — a distinction Shaheen keeps live for two centuries. And he gives each
incoming family’s origin, which is always somewhere else.

**The platform does not choose.** This is a live question about who is a Regular Member,
not a design question. It goes to the **Membership Committee** and the **Legal Advisor**
with both readings and the page numbers.

## 4 · The 1944 clan census — the check that has power

The book tabulates Ramallah’s population by clan twice, in 1905 and 1944, both “according
to its eight clans.” The 1944 count is from ration cards, p. 28.

| clan | 1944 | share | record | share | Δ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ibrahim | 775 | 15.9% | 3,337 | 14.0% | −1.8 |
| Yousef | 750 | 15.4% | 3,593 | 15.1% | −0.2 |
| Jaghab | 675 | 13.8% | 3,176 | 13.4% | −0.5 |
| Shakara | 660 | 13.5% | 3,057 | 12.9% | −0.7 |
| Sharaka | 650 | 13.3% | 3,356 | 14.1% | +0.8 |
| ‘Awwaad | 625 | 12.8% | 3,335 | 14.0% | +1.2 |
| Jirius | 550 | 11.3% | 2,955 | 12.4% | +1.2 |
| Hassaan | 200 | 4.1% | 966 | 4.1% | 0.0 |

Mean absolute difference in share **0.80 points**, worst case 1.8. Hassaan is the smallest
clan by a wide margin in both — 4.1% then, 4.1% now.

**The 581 Azzouz are left out of both sides, deliberately.** Folding them into Jaghab on the
strength of p. 22 and then offering the fit as corroboration of p. 22 would be circular, and
it makes the fit *worse* (0.96 against 0.80). The best-fitting home for them would be
Ibrahim, which is an artefact and nothing else. So they sit outside the table, where they
cannot flatter it.

**The book’s Shakara is the roster’s Shukair** and the database’s `SHAK`. The row matches on
the count (3,057 is exactly the SHAK series) and on position in both censuses, but the two
documents spell the clan differently and the platform does not quietly pick one.

Not full independence, since the genealogy draws on Shaheen. But a ration-card headcount of
residents is a different instrument from a genealogy of descendants, and the two agreeing
this closely means the record’s clan proportions have not drifted through three generations
of emigration to nine American cities. That is a real property of the file.

**Eight is stated three times over:** p. 22 outright; the **1905** census, which is introduced
“according to its eight clans” (the 1944 table used here is introduced only as “a 1944 census
based on the ration cards” and carries the same eight rows); and the flight after the ‘Azzouz
Incident on p. 33, which names eight and no more — Shakara, Sharaka, Jaghab, Yousef,
Hasasneh, ‘Awwaad, Ibrahim, Jirius.

## 5 · What the book does not contain

> “But once a Ramallah girl marries a man not from Ramallah, her genealogy ceases.”
> — preface, p. 12

Shaheen states it plainly. The same preface records that on returning in 1958 he realised
trees omitting the womenfolk would not be complete and began again; that women appear twice
in the details, as daughters and again when they marry; and that when a family has only
daughters their names **do** appear on the diagrams as if they were sons. He built in the
exception himself.

**The rule is visible in the file, and it is conditional exactly as he wrote it.** Take the
people the book placed in a line — whose own parents are recorded — and ask whether the
line continues through them.

| | n | line continues |
|---|---|---|
| married daughter, husband also in a line | 3,195 | **86.8%** |
| married daughter, husband not | 2,243 | **34.9%** |
| married son, wife also in a line | 3,134 | 91.2% |
| married son, wife not | 2,951 | 81.1% |

Marriage itself is even — 54.7% of sons, 54.0% of daughters — so the record does not lose
daughters at marriage. **51.9 points against 10.1.** For a son, whom he marries barely
matters. For a daughter it is most of the effect. That is not a general under-recording of
women; it is the specific rule Shaheen wrote down, showing up as an interaction, with the
sons as the control that rules the general explanation out.

**2,243 daughters married outside the recorded universe.** At the rate of daughters who
married inside it, **1,164 more** of their lines would be recorded as continuing. They are
not — and each one is a branch of Ramallah descent a search of Shaheen will not find.

*Caveat:* “not in a line” is a proxy for “not from Ramallah”; a Ramallah man missing his own
parents is misclassified as an outsider, which **dilutes** the effect rather than
manufacturing it. Figures are computed from the 28,226-person file at build time
(`tree/gap.py`), not typed in.

Shaheen names a second limit on p. 21: from the founders “we can go back another six or
eight generations. **The links in between are lost, and there is no way to recover them.**”

**Consequence.** Absence from Shaheen is not a finding against an applicant. It is why
**By-Law 4.2.1** exists, why the Membership Committee may accept other proof, and why the
tree is filed as **evidence for a human decision, never a verdict** (Decision 16).

## 6 · Two claims withdrawn — kept on the screen

**“10 of 10 — nobody arranged this.”** A table of ten Chapter 2 families all agreeing with
the roster, offered as independent corroboration. One row was **invented** — Harb → Yousef
appears nowhere in the book, so “Yousef” could only have come from the roster the check was
testing. Two rows, **Ayyoub** and **Mikhail**, were not arrivals but existing ‘Awwaad
families conscripted to Acre and returned. One clan, **Hishmeh**, was our inference. And the
check had **no power to fail**: the master genealogy’s own source of record *is* Shaheen, so
a roster drawn from Shaheen scores 100% by construction.

**“38.1% of married women have no parents recorded.”** Offered as the record’s footprint of
the marry-out rule. It measured **the opposite population**: a Ramallah daughter is by
construction the daughter of a man in the book, so she has parents recorded and cannot be in
that 38.1%. The women it counted married *in* — 98.8% of them to a man who does have parents
recorded — and they do not stop: 78.5% have children recorded, a higher rate than married
women who do have parents.

Both are on the clans tab, under “Two claims this page made yesterday, withdrawn”. A
platform that only shows its wins is not showing its method.

## 7 · One census, and three that disagreed

The clans tab was carrying **three different headcounts of the same clans**: the roster
cards totalled 26,474, the 1944 census panel 24,356, and the tree tab 28,226 less 3,870
unclanned — also 24,356. The cards were wrong: a looser prefix match counted anyone holding
two reference codes twice, and produced more clan members than the file has placed people.

One rule now, everywhere (`tree/clandata.py`): **a person belongs to the clan of their first
reference code.** That places **23,775** in the eight clans, **581** on the Azzouz line, and
leaves **3,870** with no clan reference at all — 28,226 exactly.

Two figures moved as a result, and both are stated on the tab:

- roster coverage is **138 of 164 family names (84%)**, not 146 of 164 (89%).
- it is described as a **consistency** check, not an independent one. The genealogy’s own
  source of record is Shaheen 1982, and so, in all likelihood, is the roster — the same
  objection that sank the withdrawn 10-of-10 claim. What it establishes is that AFRP’s two
  documents have not drifted apart, which is worth knowing and is not a validation.

The Jaghab card also used to render **Azzouz greyed out with “not found in the record”**,
while a panel six inches below counted 581 Azzouz. It now shows 92 with the reason: they are
under the AZZ series, not under Jaghab, because the clan merged and the reference codes never
did.

## 8 · Two build defects fixed while here

**Generator order was load-bearing and undeclared.** `gen-clans.py` inserts its script before
`</body>` and knows nothing about the generators that wrap its renderer. Re-running it after
`gen-shaheen.py` would have silently blanked the entire Shaheen surface, with no error — the
wrapper would capture an undefined base and then be overwritten. The wrapper now installs
lazily and marks itself, so order cannot break it, and `build/build.sh` fixes the order anyway.

**The inline-`<b>` leak, fixed page-wide at last.** `.qrow__main b{display:block}` turns every
bold word inside a row description into its own line. It has been patched four times in
individual places. One rule now covers the whole page: `.qrow__main span b{display:inline}`,
plus the same for nested spans, which was breaking every `AZZ` code onto a line of its own.

## 9 · Also reconciled

Older statements on the platform that were inconsistent with the book, and are fixed:

| | was | now |
|---|---|---|
| clan count | “The nine clans” | **Eight clans, nine prefixes**, with the reason |
| founder, in the prose | Rashid Al-Haddad | **Rashed Haddad**, as the book spells him |
| founder, in the diagram | Haddad, Hassan, Shukair, Ibrahim, Jeries | **Haddad, Ibrahim, Jirius, Shukair, Hassaan** — the rewrite had reached the paragraph and not the pills six lines below it |
| settlement | “reached Ramallah around 1517” asserted | the sixteenth century; 1517 kept only where the tree already labels it *tradition*, against Shaheen’s “about 1550” |
| multi-clan names | “Nine names” | **eight as printed**, with the folded count of nine explained rather than asserted as a mismatch |
| the tree tab’s clan panel | “All descended from Rashed El-Haddadeen” | “Founded by Rashed Haddad’s line, joined by others” — the old subtitle contradicted the attachment reading two tabs away |

And one disclosure lost in a rebuild is restored to the tree tab: **“Who can see this”**,
which records in writing that the platform puts roughly 27,000 living people’s names and
family structure in front of anyone who pays to join. It went missing when that screen was
rebuilt, and only a test noticed. Restored, narrowed for Decision 28 — dates are gated now,
names and structure are not.

## 10 · Still unread

The **Arabic section** — the genealogies themselves, with the family-tree diagrams — has not
been read; the English section is 71 pages of history and preface.
`1982_Shahin_family_index.json` maps **105 Arabic family names to page numbers** and is the
obvious next step: it would let the platform answer *“is this family in the book, and on
what page”* — the literal text of 4.1.1 — instead of inferring it from the GEDCOM.

The same folder holds `2005_Shaheen_A_Pictorial_History_of_Ramallah_Part_II.pdf`,
`198x_Farhan_Qissat_Ramallah_wal-Bira.pdf`, the 1922 and 1931 Palestine censuses, and
`AFRP_Register_Names_1553_1562.csv` — 117 named household heads from the Ottoman registers
of 1553–54 and 1562, the earliest naming evidence for the area.
